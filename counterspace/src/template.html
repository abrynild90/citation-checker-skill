Counterspace, 1957–2026: the weapons and the law that trailed them

Counterspace is the name for weapons and methods that can attack or disable satellites. This page shows them, and the laws and policies that followed, against the same years, from 1957 to 2026.

Tests that destroy a satellite have paused since November 2021. Jamming, lasers and cyber operations have not.

The main source, and orbits in plain words

The Secure World Foundation (SWF), whose 2026 report is the main source of the facts here, finds that “only non-destructive capabilities are actively being used against satellites in current military operations” (Executive Summary, p. xxiii).

Low Earth orbit (LEO) reaches up to about 2,000 km above the Earth. Global Positioning System (GPS) satellites sit in medium Earth orbit (MEO), about 20,200 km up. Broadcast and communications satellites sit in the geostationary belt (GEO), about 35,786 km up.

        Attacks that leave satellites in orbit

        Each bar is a campaign of jamming, lasers or hacking that leaves the satellites it targets in orbit. Each dot is a single event, and an arrow means the campaign is still going.

        Key to the bars and dots

          The rules that govern these operations are soft law (expert manuals that are not binding) or carry military exemptions, such as Article 48 of the Constitution of the International Telecommunication Union (ITU), the United Nations agency for radio and telecommunications.

          Satellites that fly close to other satellites

          Each shape is one close approach (also called a rendezvous or proximity operation) between a satellite and another object in orbit, placed at its start date. Arrows show those still going.

          A close approach is not an attack. Most are inspections, servicing missions or technology tests.

          What the source does and does not say

          A close approach, or proximity operation, is two objects in orbit coming near each other. The Secure World Foundation (SWF) records that an operation took place, not why. SWF itself is cautious about intent, using words such as “possibly” and “appeared to”; an outlined shape shows those entries.

            Which states hold which counterspace capabilities, by decade

            Each bar counts how many states have a way to attack or interfere with satellites in one decade. Only the 2020s bar rests on the Secure World Foundation’s (SWF) own assessment; earlier decades are reconstructed.

            Reading the 2020s bar: the low number counts capabilities that have been tested or used, and the high number adds those still in development. This chart does not use the shared years.

            Key to the bar patterns

              How we classified these

              For the 2020s, the states and capabilities come from SWF’s 2026 assessment of 13 countries. Whether a capability counts as demonstrated (tested or used) or as still developing is our reading of SWF’s text, so the range bar is the uncertain part: its low end counts demonstrated capabilities only, and its high end adds those still developing. Dotted segments are capabilities we class as developing where SWF’s country table has no data. Decades before 2020 are reconstructed from SWF’s test tables and country chapters; SWF did not assess them.

              Direct-ascent anti-satellite weapons are missiles launched from Earth to hit a satellite. Co-orbital means a satellite that maneuvers close to another. Electronic warfare means jamming and spoofing. Directed energy means lasers and similar beams. Cyber means attacks through computers and networks. The full rules are in Sources and method.

                  Where every fact comes from

                  Almost every weapon and operation comes from one report, the Secure World Foundation’s Global Counterspace Capabilities (9th edition, April 2026). The laws and policies come from the treaties and documents themselves. To check a fact, open the data table behind its chart, note the source and page reference for the entry, then find that source in the full list under “Read the sources and method”.
